# The inbox

Not every thought arrives ready to become a piece of work. Mid-way through building one thing you notice another that is broken, or an idea worth exploring later, or a small change that ought to happen sometime. The inbox is where such thoughts go so you can keep working.

You capture by asking, in any conversation: "log that as an idea", "log this bug", "log a quick-fix". The system writes a short note and carries on with what you were doing. Capture is deliberately narrow. It will not read your code, judge feasibility, diagnose the bug or suggest an approach; it writes your thought down faithfully and nothing more, so the capture is fast and honest and the thinking happens later, if you decide the thought is worth it.

```
   ideas        something you might build or explore
   bugs         something broken: symptoms, conditions, impact
   quick-fixes  a small mechanical change: what, where, why
```

Notes wait in the inbox until you act on them from the start menu, whose `i/inbox` row appears whenever there is something waiting. There you build a working set by selecting one or more notes, and whatever you choose applies to the whole set: read them in full, promote them into a piece of work, or archive them out of the way. Promotion carries the notes into discovery as the origin of a new piece of work, where they become its seeds, the recorded reason the work exists. It is offered only when every note in the set is the same kind, because a piece of work is one kind with one journey. Archiving has no such restriction, and archived notes are not gone: they can be restored to the inbox or deleted for good from the archive view.

Seeds are distinct from imports. A seed is the note the work grew out of; an import is a file you shared to inform the work, such as notes, a design document, a screenshot or an error report. You can share a file at the start of a piece of work or at any point in its early conversations by giving the path, and it is kept with the work and linked from the document that discussed it. One is the spark and the other is fuel, and both are read early and remembered.
