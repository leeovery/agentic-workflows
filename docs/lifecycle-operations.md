# Lifecycle operations

Work does not always run cleanly from start to finish in the shape it began. A feature outgrows itself; another turns out to belong inside a larger effort; something gets shelved and later revived; a topic proves to be a dead end. The system treats all of this as normal and gives you a small set of operations to reshape work as your understanding of it changes. You reach them through the manage menu on a work unit, or through an epic's dashboard; each is a deliberate action you invoke, not something the system does behind your back.

## The status of a work unit

Every work unit is in one of three states. It is **in-progress** by default while you are working on it. It becomes **completed** when its pipeline finishes — set automatically at the end of the last phase, or by hand when you decide it is done. It becomes **cancelled** when you abandon it. Active work is what you see by default; completed and cancelled work is kept behind a "view completed and cancelled" door so it does not clutter the day-to-day, but it is not lost. Cancelling a work unit also removes its material from the [knowledge base](knowledge-base.md), because an abandoned decision should not resurface as advice on future work.

Closing a work unit — completing it, cancelling it, or absorbing it into an epic — also clears its scratch working files: the per-phase working notes the system keeps only so an interrupted session can pick up where it left off. Finished work keeps its real artifacts and its history; the scaffolding goes.

None of these states is a one-way door. A completed or cancelled work unit can be **reactivated** back to in-progress — and reactivating a cancelled one restores its material to the knowledge base, since it is live thinking again. Any working files it needs are simply rebuilt as you go. Completed work can also be revisited phase by phase without reactivating the whole unit, when you just want to amend an earlier phase.

## Pivot: a feature that grew

Sometimes a feature turns out to be bigger than one coherent thing. What looked like a single unit of work keeps fanning out into distinct concerns, each with its own decisions to make — which is the definition of an epic, not a feature. **Pivot** converts the feature into an epic in place, so you keep everything already done and gain the multi-topic structure the work now needs. You would reach for it the moment a feature's discussion starts feeling like several discussions trying to happen at once. After pivoting you can carry straight on in the epic, or step back to where you were.

## Absorb: a feature that belongs elsewhere

The opposite situation: you have a standalone feature that, on reflection, belongs inside a larger epic already underway. **Absorb** merges the feature into an in-progress epic as one of its topics, moving its discussion (and any research, [experiments](experiments.md), seeds, and imports) across, then deletes the now-redundant feature. Everything a topic holds travels whole — the Discussion Map, research's thread register, the standing do-not-report calls — and an experiment series with its records, verdicts, and a still-open experiment's hold on its conversation, so a discussion that was waiting on evidence is still waiting on it inside the epic. Its git history remains as the record of where it came from. Absorption is guarded — the feature must have a discussion, must not have progressed past discussion into a spec or beyond, and there must be an in-progress epic to absorb it into — because merging half-specified work would tangle two pipelines together. When the guards are not met, the system tells you which one blocked and touches nothing. Reach for absorb when you realise a feature you have been discussing is really one facet of a bigger initiative.

## Cancelling and reviving epic topics

Inside an epic, individual topics have their own lifecycle, separate from the epic as a whole. You can **cancel** a single topic when it turns out to be a dead end or no longer worth pursuing, without touching the rest of the epic — whether it has been started or not. A cancelled topic stays visible in the epic's display as the record that it was raised and declined, but drops out of progress counts and stops holding up the phases that depend on it. Cancel works on the thing you see on the dashboard, never on its parts. Cancelling a topic takes everything under its name together — its research, its discussion, and any [experiments](experiments.md) still open, which end as abandoned with the cancellation recorded as their reason so the register stays an honest history; a grouping the system had only proposed over that discussion is discarded with it, and the confirmation says so. Cancelling a specification takes the specification and its plan together, and hands its source discussions back to be regrouped or cancelled in their own right. There is no cancelling half a topic.

Later work protects earlier work. A topic whose discussion a started specification is built from cannot be cancelled while that specification stands: the cancel menu still shows the topic, with the specification named as the reason and no way to pick it, and cancelling the specification first frees it. Once implementation has begun under a specification, that specification cannot be cancelled at all — code in the tree is fixed forward through new work, and abandoning the whole effort is the epic's own cancel. Every row is shown, locked ones included, so a cancel that is refused is never a missing option with an invisible cause; and the confirmation before every cancel names exactly what it takes, and nothing more is ever taken.

**Reactivating** a topic restores what the cancel took — its prior statuses (a topic that had never been started comes back never started), its place in the order (unless another topic has since taken that number, in which case the next sequencing pass seats it), and, if it had completed thinking work, that work's place in the knowledge base. Three things do not come back, because they were never the cancel's to hold: an experiment the cancel ended stays abandoned on the register, a grouping the system had only proposed is gone and the next analysis proposes afresh, and a roadmap item the cancel handed back to waiting stays waiting until it is pulled again. A specification comes back on two conditions, and the reactivate menu names whichever fails on its row, with no way to pick it: its source topics must themselves be live — reactivate a cancelled topic first — and no other specification may have taken its sources in the meantime; when one has, the way back is to regroup at the specification entry rather than revive the old grouping over discussions now owned elsewhere. Reactivating a specification also discards any grouping the system had proposed over its sources, since those discussions are accounted for again. This topic-level control exists only for epics, because only an epic has topics distinct from the work unit itself; for every other type, cancelling the topic and cancelling the work unit are the same thing.

## Postponing a topic to the roadmap

Not every topic you stop working on is one you are declining. Some are simply not this epic's to build — wanted, valid, and belonging to a later release. **Postponing** a topic sends it to the [product roadmap](roadmap.md) under a horizon you name, and it differs from cancelling in every way that matters: nothing is deleted, nothing moves on disk, and the topic leaves whole — its research, its discussion, its experiments and its brief all stay where they are, pointed at from the roadmap item now waiting in their place. Its thinking does drop out of the epic's search results while it waits, because conclusions on hold should not surface as advice on the work still running; it comes back with the topic.

The item is born under the topic's own name when the epic raised the topic itself; when the topic came from the roadmap in the first place, that same item simply goes back to waiting, in the horizon you have just chosen. Either way it remembers where it came from, which is why the way back is a pull rather than a reactivate — offered on the epic's own dashboard, beside the postpone row that sent the topic away, for as long as the item is still waiting. Pull it forward into the epic that postponed it and the topic returns exactly as it left — its prior statuses, its place in the order, its thinking back in the knowledge base. Pull it into a different epic and you get a fresh topic pointing at the earlier record: its first conversation reads that record in full and decides again, rather than inheriting decisions a new context ought to re-examine.

What holds a postpone shut is named on the row itself. A specification already being built from the topic's discussion: a topic past specification is past "not yet", so cancelling the specification comes first if that is really what you mean. An [experiment](experiments.md) still running under it, which you conclude or abandon yourself rather than have the system abandon for you. And a roadmap item already holding the topic's name that belongs to something else, which the roadmap's own rename or remove clears. A topic closed as a dead end is turned back too, but for a different reason: its question was answered and nothing carries forward under its name, so sending it to a list of things still to do would say something untrue — reopen it first, and then it can wait like anything else. Afterwards the topic belongs to the roadmap — it can be re-bucketed freely, and removing it from there is the single door to "actually never", cancelling the epic's row in the same move.

Four routes reach it, all the same act: the postpone row on the epic's dashboard, saying so mid-conversation in any phase ("postpone this", "the loyalty topic is v2"), asking for it in a discovery session, and the third option on the gate that reviews the topics the system has noticed are missing — for a gap that is real but belongs to a later release.

## Promoting a spec to a standing document

Occasionally a specification you were writing for one topic turns out to describe a project-wide pattern rather than a single unit of work — a convention the rest of the codebase should follow. Such a spec can be **promoted** into its own cross-cutting concern, where it becomes a standing document of record instead of a step toward one build. This is the same recognition [cross-cutting work](work-types.md) is built around, applied after the fact when a piece of work reveals itself to be broader than it looked.

## Revisiting finished work

Completed phases reopen cleanly — from a work unit's menu you can step back into any earlier finished phase to amend it, without reactivating or restarting anything. What makes this safe is that the system tracks what the revisit sits underneath. The moment you reopen something another phase was built on, that downstream phase is marked **input moved**: the cue appears on its status rows and menu entries, forward routing points back at it instead of past it, and nothing built on the stale input is recommended until it is dealt with. The mark clears the next time you enter the affected phase, where you are shown what changed upstream and the phase reconciles it (a discussion marked because research moved beneath it holds the mark until the research has landed) — and that reconciliation decides whether the next phase down needs marking in turn. The ripple travels one phase at a time, always in the open. If the revisit changed nothing, each clear is a quick touch; if it changed something real, the rework it prompts is rework you wanted. A live mark also survives the operations on this page — absorb a feature mid-reconcile and the mark travels into the epic with it.

## Moving ahead of yourself, safely

One more behaviour is worth naming because it feels like a guardrail. When you navigate forward in an epic out of turn, the system warns you. Starting specification while discussions are still open counts what is unfinished. From planning onward it reads the epic's **build order** — the suggested sequence over its topics — and names the topics sitting ahead of your selection that are still unplanned or unbuilt, so the warning tells you exactly what you are stepping past. For most transitions it is a warning, not a wall: you can proceed anyway, and if the earlier work changes what you have done, the system re-analyses and surfaces the mismatch when you return. Specification is the one deliberate exception. It is built from the settled discussion record, so the grouping analysis refuses to run while any discussion is still open, and a specification whose own source discussion has reopened is blocked — visible, with the discussions that hold it named — until that discussion re-concludes. Everywhere else, an informed nudge serves you better than a locked door; there, a spec built on moving ground would be a false contract.
